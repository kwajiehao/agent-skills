# Spaced repetition for an adult language beginner

**Learn a little, recall it without looking, and revisit it on later days.** Spaced repetition distributes reviews over time; *retrieval practice* means trying to bring an answer to mind before checking it. Combining them is a useful way to retain vocabulary. A 2022 synthesis of 48 second-language experiments involving 3,411 participants found substantial benefits from spacing, although results varied by task and study design. Remembering cards is only one part of learning to communicate. [Kim and Webb’s language-learning meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479).

Memory becomes harder to access with time. A later review makes you retrieve or relearn the material instead of relying on its immediate familiarity. Retrieval itself can improve later recall: in a university-student experiment using Swahili–English word pairs, continuing to test already-recalled words improved retention one week later more than continuing to study them. This supports returning to words even after getting them right once. [Karpicke and Roediger’s retrieval experiment](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_Science.pdf).

```text
Learn a word and its meaning
             |
      Wait between reviews
             |
 Try to recall WITHOUT looking
             |
     Check the correct answer
             |
      Plan the next review
       /                 \
Remembered            Forgotten
Try a longer gap      Relearn; use a shorter gap
       \                 /
        Repeat on later days
```

**Takeaway:** each review is a chance to practise remembering. This is an illustrative learning loop, not measured data or a complete explanation of brain biology. The recall benefit comes from the [retrieval experiment](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_Science.pdf); adjusting gaps is a practical application of [research showing that review timing matters](https://labs.biology.ucsd.edu/rifkin/courses/bieb100/f14/Cepeda_et_al_2008_Psychological_Science_Spacing_Effects_in_Learning_A_Temporal_Ridgeline_of_Optimal_Retention.pdf).

For your first two weeks, try this manageable routine. The quantities and schedule below are practical starting suggestions, not experimentally established prescriptions:

1. **Choose five useful words or short phrases** from your beginner lesson—greetings, food, directions, or things you want to say. Learn the meaning and check the pronunciation before making cards.
2. **Make one clear question per card.** An illustrative Spanish card: front, “Say ‘I want water’”; back, “Quiero agua,” with a pronunciation reference. Add a separate card asking what the Spanish phrase means if you also want to practise comprehension.
3. **Review for about ten minutes daily.** Say or write your answer before revealing it; then check meaning, wording, and pronunciation. Review older cards before adding new ones. If reviews become burdensome, pause new cards.
4. **Revisit successful cards after roughly 1, 3, 7, and 14 days from learning.** If you forget, check the answer, try again after other cards, and return sooner. A notebook with review dates is enough; an existing flashcard scheduler can handle the dates too.
5. **Use the language outside cards.** Listen to a short beginner dialogue, read a simple passage, and use a reviewed phrase in a conversation or written sentence. Check whether you can understand and produce it beyond the original prompt.

There is no universal best interval. A large adult memory experiment found that effective gaps depended on how long people needed to retain the material; excessively long gaps also reduced performance. It studied facts, so it does not establish an exact language-card schedule. [Cepeda and colleagues’ spacing experiment](https://labs.biology.ucsd.edu/rifkin/courses/bieb100/f14/Cepeda_et_al_2008_Psychological_Science_Spacing_Effects_in_Learning_A_Temporal_Ridgeline_of_Optimal_Retention.pdf). The language meta-analysis found no clear advantage for expanding over equal intervals. Consistent spaced practice matters more than treating the sample schedule as mandatory. [Kim and Webb](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12479).

*Research checked 13 October 2026: a focused online review of language-spacing evidence and original memory experiments, not a systematic review or an app comparison. The meta-analysis’s abstract was accessible; its full text and supplement were not. The practical routine is an application of the findings, and these sources do not establish that flashcard practice alone produces conversational fluency.*
