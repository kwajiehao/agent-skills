# Research method

## Search to answer the brief

Convert the question into evidence needs. An explanation needs a reliable description of the mechanism and its boundaries; a comparison needs common criteria and comparable conditions; an intervention question needs evidence of outcomes rather than testimonials. Do not inherit the user's suggested answer as a finding.

For a substantial inquiry, sketch subquestions and unresolved assumptions before searching. Use short queries to discover terminology and major sources, then narrow by relevant dates, geography, populations, versions, outcomes, or study design. Search for evidence that could disprove your current interpretation as well as support it. Follow citations and references backward to original evidence and forward to corrections or newer results where relevant.

Open and inspect the material you rely on. Search snippets locate sources; they are not a substitute for reading them. If an important source is inaccessible, find an accessible original or corroborating source, or retain the claim as unverified. Do not invent its contents. Search failure does not establish that evidence does not exist.

For time-sensitive claims, distinguish publication/update date, the period measured, and the event date. Check current official documentation, policies, prices, or data rather than relying on an older summary. For historical questions, preserve the requested time frame instead of silently substituting present conditions.

## Choose evidence suitable for the claim

Evaluate authority, methods, relevance, independence, recency, and incentives together. Primary sources are especially useful for what an organization officially documents, what a study measured, or what a dataset contains. A vendor's documentation can establish a feature but cannot by itself establish superiority or real-world effectiveness. Independent evaluation matters for those claims. A well-conducted evidence synthesis may be more informative than one original study.

Several articles repeating the same press release or dataset are one evidence chain, not independent corroboration. Track the underlying origin. For disputed or consequential claims, seek independent support or a strong challenge where available. Do not give weak evidence equal weight merely to appear balanced.

For empirical claims, inspect who or what was studied, sample and selection, methods, measured outcomes, effect size, uncertainty, and applicability. Distinguish association from causation and evidence of absence from absence of evidence. Preprints, anecdotes, simulations, vendor benchmarks, and controlled trials answer different questions; describe relevant limitations without dismissing them automatically.

## Keep claim-level evidence notes

Use a small table or equivalent notes. Expand only as needed:

| Claim or subquestion | Source and locator | Evidence, date, and context | Interpretation and limits | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What needs establishing | Actual URL plus section/page/table | Short passage or exact data; relevant period/population | How it answers the question; independence or applicability concerns | Supported, inferred, disputed, or unknown |

Keep exact values and units separate from paraphrases. Record retrieved dates for changing sources. Notes should preserve enough context to check the claim later, not copy whole pages. If work may span turns, save a compact brief and evidence record in an appropriate local workspace; do not expose private material through public searches or publishing.

## Reconcile, then explain

When sources disagree, check whether they measure the same thing under the same conditions. Differences may arise from definitions, dates, geography, populations, baselines, versions, methodology, or incentives. Explain a resolved difference; when it remains unresolved, present the competing findings and the reason confidence is limited.

Weight conclusions by the quality and applicability of the supporting evidence, not the number of links. For qualitative confidence, give a reason such as “supported by several independent studies, but only in short trials.” Confidence belongs to a claim, not automatically to the whole report. Do not call an informal judgment a formal GRADE assessment or a quick web review a systematic review.

Recommendations require an extra step: relate evidence to the reader's constraints and explain the tradeoff. Label assumptions and judgments. If the evidence supports several choices under different conditions, make the recommendation conditional. If it does not distinguish the options, identify the measurement or experiment that would.

## Coverage and stopping

Review the brief, subquestions, source notes, and strongest competing explanation before stopping. Close a question only with relevant evidence or an explicit gap. Deepen research when a missing source, contradiction, or poorly matched population could materially alter the answer. Stop when those issues are adequately addressed and new searches add repetition rather than decision-relevant information.

In the report's method note, describe the actual coverage and important omissions. Use “not established in the sources reviewed” rather than claiming something is impossible or universally absent. Under a time or access limit, provide the supported answer and identify what remains open.
